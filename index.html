1Project Overview

Part 1 · Research

Your group investigates how Bayes' Theorem is used in a real computing system and finds credible values for the probabilities involved.

Part 2 · Application

You build and solve your own Bayesian model using those researched values, interpret the results, and discuss the ethical issues of probabilistic inference.

The project includes a weekly consultation checkpoint and a short presentation.

P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) / P(B)

2Objectives

By the end of the project, you should be able to:

  1. Research how Bayes' Theorem is used in a real computing system, using credible sources.
  2. Translate a real-world computing scenario into events, priors, and conditional probabilities, and justify the values with research.
  3. Use the Law of Total Probability to compute P(evidence) from a partition of the sample space.
  4. Apply Bayes' Theorem to compute and interpret posterior probabilities.
  5. Represent the solution with a probability tree diagram and a computation table.
  6. Evaluate how changing the prior, sensitivity, or false-positive rate affects the result.
  7. Compare your results with findings reported in your sources and state the limitations of your model.
  8. Discuss ethical issues such as bias, false positives, and privacy in probabilistic inference.

3Task Description

Each member chooses one scenario below: Options A to C, or an approved research-based option listed after Option C.

Option A — Spam Filtering

An email system receives messages that are either Spam (S) or Not Spam (S′). The system flags a message based on keywords (e.g., “free”, “winner”, “claim”, “urgent”). Your group must:

  • Define the prior P(S) using a stated proportion of spam in a mailbox.
  • Choose at least three keywords and assign P(keyword | S) and P(keyword | S′). Justify your values (a sample of at least 50 messages your group creates or collects, with personal details removed, is acceptable).
  • Compute P(S | keyword) for each keyword using Bayes' Theorem.
  • Compute P(S | two or more keywords) under a stated independence assumption (naive Bayes), and explain when this assumption is unrealistic.

Option B — Diagnostic Testing (Software or Device Screening)

A screening tool tests for a condition D (e.g., a disease in a health-screening app, or a defect in a manufactured device), with a sensitivity P(+ | D) and a false-positive rate P(+ | D′). Your group must:

  • Define the prevalence P(D) with a stated source or assumption.
  • Compute P(+) using the Law of Total Probability.
  • Compute the positive predictive value P(D | +) and the negative predictive value P(D′ | −).
  • Show how P(D | +) changes when prevalence is 1%, 5%, 20%, and 50%, and explain the base-rate effect.
  • Analyze a second, independent test and compute P(D | +, +).

Option C — Fault Detection in a System

A system has three machines (or servers/modules) M₁, M₂, M₃ that produce a share of total output (or handle a share of requests), each with a different defect or failure rate. When a defect or failure is found, determine which source is most likely responsible. Your group must:

  • Define P(Mᵢ) for each source and P(Defect | Mᵢ).
  • Compute P(Defect) using the Law of Total Probability.
  • Compute P(Mᵢ | Defect) for all three sources and rank them.
  • Recommend which source to inspect first and explain why, then show how the ranking changes if a source's workload share is doubled.

4Research Component

Your project must be grounded in research. Before building the model, investigate how Bayesian reasoning is used in systems like the one you chose, and use what you find to justify your probabilities.

Research questions

  1. How does a real system in this area use Bayes' Theorem or Bayesian reasoning, and what problem does it solve?
  2. What are realistic values for the prior, the sensitivity (or likelihood), and the false-positive rate, according to published sources?
  3. What assumptions do real systems make (e.g., independence between features), and where do those assumptions fail?
  4. What are the documented consequences of errors, such as false alarms and missed detections, in this area?
  5. How do your computed results compare with the values and findings reported in your sources?

Source requirements

  • At least four credible sources: at least two academic (journal articles, conference papers, or textbooks) and at least one technical documentation page or industry report.
  • Sources must be real, accessible, and cited in APA format. Encyclopedias and blogs may support background reading but do not count toward the minimum.
  • Prefer sources from the last 10 years, unless a classic source is needed.
  • Where no published value exists, state your assumption and label it clearly as an assumption.

Research outputs

② Annotated bibliography

5Requirements and Deliverables

Each group member submits the following:

1 · Written report

6–8 pages, excluding the cover page:
  • Problem statement and scenario chosen
  • Research summary and annotated bibliography
  • Events and assumptions
  • Sourced table of probabilities
  • Probability tree diagram
  • Step-by-step solution (Total Probability and Bayes)
  • Sensitivity analysis (≥ 3 varied values, table or graph)
  • Plain-language interpretation and comparison with sources
  • Limitations of the model
  • Ethical discussion
  • References in APA format

2 · Computation file

Spreadsheet or short program (Java optional) that reproduces the computations and lets the user change the input values. Code or formulas must be readable and commented.

3 · Presentation

8–10 minutes plus 3–5 minutes Q&A, with slides or a poster, including one slide on the research findings.

4 · Contribution sheet

Individual contribution sheet listing each member's tasks, signed by all members.

6Ethical Discussion Component

The report must include a section of at least one page that addresses:

Bias

Could the priors or training data be biased or unrepresentative? Who might be unfairly affected?

False positives & negatives

What are the real-world consequences of each type of error in your scenario? Which is more costly?

Privacy

What data would be needed, and how should it be collected, stored, and protected? Refer to the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173) where applicable.

Transparency & accountability

How should the system's limits be communicated to its users?

8Guidelines

  • All probability values must be stated clearly with a source or a reasonable assumption. Do not use real personal or medical data of identifiable people.
  • Every source must be real and verifiable. Invented sources, statistics, or quotations are treated as academic dishonesty.
  • Show every step of the computation. Answers without solutions receive no credit for that part.
  • Keep at least four decimal places during computation and round final answers to three or four decimal places (or as a percentage).
  • Cite all references (textbooks, articles, datasets, documentation) in a consistent format such as APA.
  • Academic integrity applies: copied work, or AI-generated work that members cannot explain, will be penalized. Every member must be able to explain any part of the project, including the research, during Q&A.
  • Late submissions are subject to the instructor's deduction policy.
